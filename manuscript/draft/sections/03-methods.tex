\section{Parameter Displacement under Stochastic Gradient Descent}
\label{sec:displacement}

Loss spikes and oscillatory updates motivate practical diagnostics that can be
computed from gradients already available during training. We develop these
diagnostics by relating parameter displacement to gradient magnitudes and
directional alignment, then connecting alignment to curvature and sampling
noise. The resulting quantities characterize when updates reinforce or cancel
one another, helping interpret constructive and destructive regimes and inform
possible interventions without computing a Hessian.

Consider the plain SGD update in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd_map_stability}, with constant
learning rate $\eta>0$. As before, $\theta_t\in\mathbb{R}^p$ denotes the
parameters after update $t$, and $g_t$ is the mean mini-batch gradient evaluated
at $\theta_{t-1}$. Summing the updates gives the displacement from initialization,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_T:=\theta_T-\theta_0=-\eta\sum_{t=1}^T g_t.
  \label{eq:displacement}
\end{equation}
For a model trained from scratch, $\theta_0$ provides a reference that has not
yet learned from the training data. After $T$ updates, the trained parameters
$\theta_T$ encode what the model has learned within that training budget.
The displacement $\Delta_T$ captures the net parameter change through which
this learning is expressed. Its squared norm therefore gives a geometric
measure of the magnitude of that change, connecting the idea of learning from
data to an observable quantity in parameter space. This motivates examining
how individual updates accumulate into the trained model. Expanding the
squared displacement separates gradient magnitudes from interactions between
their directions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \|\Delta_T\|^2
  ={}&\eta^2\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|^2\\
     &+2\eta^2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}g_u^\top g_t.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:magnitude-interaction}
\end{equation}
The terms $\|g_t\|^2$ are nonnegative and determine the squared lengths of
individual SGD steps through $\eta^2\|g_t\|^2$. In full-batch GD, a larger
gradient norm indicates a steeper local loss and a larger first-order decrease
along the negative gradient. Mini-batch gradient norms also reflect sampling
noise, and the actual loss change depends on curvature. The cross-step terms
$g_u^\top g_t$ describe how these steps interact: positive inner products
reinforce the accumulated displacement, whereas negative inner products cancel
part of it. Large gradient norms can therefore coexist with strong
cancellation along the training trajectory.

To separate directional alignment from gradient magnitudes, define the cosine
similarity between any two nonzero gradients and its consecutive-step form:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \rho_{u,t}&:=\frac{g_u^\top g_t}{\|g_u\|\|g_t\|},
       &&1\leq u<t\leq T,\\
  \rho_t&:=\rho_{t-1,t}, &&t=2,\ldots,T.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Substituting these similarities into Eq.~\eqref{eq:magnitude-interaction}
expresses the squared displacement entirely through gradient norms and
directional alignment:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\Delta_T\|^2={}&\eta^2\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|^2\\
 &+2\eta^2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}
    \|g_u\|\|g_t\|\rho_{u,t}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:magnitude-alignment}
\end{equation}
If either gradient is zero, its pairwise contribution is taken as zero.
For fixed gradient norms, positive $\rho_{u,t}$ increases the squared net
displacement, negative $\rho_{u,t}$ contributes cancellation, and orthogonal
gradients have no pairwise contribution. Gradient magnitudes determine the
lengths of the steps, while their alignment determines how those steps
accumulate. In practice, $\rho_t$ provides an online diagnostic of this
interaction between successive updates: persistent positive or negative
values characterize locally reinforcing or alternating-gradient regimes.

\subsection{Directional Curvature and Consecutive Alignment}

To interpret the observed alignment, use the gradient-noise decomposition in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd_noise_decomposition},
$g_t=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1})+\xi_t$.
Suppose the full objective $\mathcal{L}$ is twice continuously differentiable
along the preceding step, and write
$H_{t-1}:=\nabla^2\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-2})$.
Expanding the full gradient around $\theta_{t-2}$ and substituting the preceding
SGD update yields
\begin{equation}
  g_t=(I-\eta H_{t-1})g_{t-1}+(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})+r_t,
  \label{eq:gradient-evolution}
\end{equation}
where $r_t$ is the Taylor remainder. Taking the inner product with $g_{t-1}$
separates the squared norm of the preceding gradient, the curvature
contribution, and the change in gradient noise. Normalize the latter two terms
by $\|g_{t-1}\|^2$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \kappa_t&:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top H_{t-1}g_{t-1}}{\|g_{t-1}\|^2},\\
  \varepsilon_t&:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})}
                            {\|g_{t-1}\|^2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kappa-epsilon}
\end{equation}
Curvature describes how the gradient changes as the parameters move. Here
$H_{t-1}g_{t-1}$ gives the local gradient response along the preceding gradient
direction, and the projection in $\kappa_t$ retains the component along that
same line. Since the SGD step is $-\eta g_{t-1}$, positive $\kappa_t$ reduces
the full gradient's component along $g_{t-1}$; a large enough change can
reverse that component. Thus, $\kappa_t$ describes curvature encountered along
the step being taken, while other directions can behave differently.

Neglecting the Taylor remainder gives the first-order relation
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\rho_t\approx\frac{\|g_{t-1}\|}{\|g_t\|}
                   (1-\eta\kappa_t+\varepsilon_t).}
  \label{eq:rho-normalized}
\end{equation}
Positive curvature reduces the next gradient's component along the preceding
gradient direction. The noise difference can reinforce or oppose that
effect on an individual step. Since the norm ratio is positive, the first-order
model predicts positive alignment when $\eta\kappa_t<1+\varepsilon_t$,
orthogonality at equality, and negative alignment when
$\eta\kappa_t>1+\varepsilon_t$. The omitted projection
$g_{t-1}^\top r_t$ can shift the sign boundary, particularly when the
first-order numerator is close to zero.

Rearranging Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho-normalized} motivates the quantity
\begin{equation}
  \eta\widehat\kappa_t
  :=1-\rho_t\frac{\|g_t\|}{\|g_{t-1}\|}+\varepsilon_t.
  \label{eq:kappa-estimate}
\end{equation}
For full-batch GD, $\xi_t=0$ and hence $\varepsilon_t=0$. The gradient-only
expression is then exactly
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\eta\widehat\kappa_t
 &=1-\rho_t\frac{\|g_t\|}{\|g_{t-1}\|}\\
 &=-\frac{g_{t-1}^\top(g_t-g_{t-1})}{\|g_{t-1}\|^2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:gd-proxy}
\end{equation}
It measures the finite-step change in the gradient projected onto its
preceding direction. Its interpretation as $\eta\kappa_t$ relies on the
Taylor approximation, whereas the equality in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gd-proxy} does not.
The transition to negative cosine occurs when this measured projected change
exceeds one. This reversal boundary differs from the quadratic GD stability
threshold $\eta\lambda_{\max}=2$.

For mini-batch SGD, the gradient-only expression remains measurable, but
$\varepsilon_t$ requires additional information about the gradient noise.
Cosine and norms alone therefore provide a projected-gradient-change
diagnostic, rather than an isolated curvature estimate. This distinction
allows the same measurements to track directional regime transitions without
attributing every reversal to curvature or identifying it with a loss spike.

\subsection{Noise Contribution under Mini-Batch Sampling}
\label{subsec:sampling-noise}

Fresh, conditionally unbiased sampling makes the new noise $\xi_t$
uncorrelated with the preceding gradient $g_{t-1}$. However, $g_{t-1}$ already
contains its own noise $\xi_{t-1}$. Consequently, assuming finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbb{E}[g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})]
  &=-\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{t-1}\|^2\\
  &\leq0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:noise-expectation}
\end{equation}
Even zero-mean sampling noise therefore contributes a nonpositive term to the
unnormalized adjacent inner product in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gradient-evolution}.
This identifies a sampling contribution that can accompany the curvature
effect in the alignment diagnostic.

To separate sampling from parameter motion, consider independent mini-batch
gradients evaluated at the same fixed parameters $\theta$. Each draw has the
form $g_t=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\xi_t$: the full-gradient component stays
the same, while the sampled component varies. Using the per-sample covariance
$\Sigma(\theta)$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad_cov_def}, a uniform batch of
$b$ examples sampled without replacement from $N>1$ examples has
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\|\xi_t\|^2
  =\frac{N-b}{b(N-1)}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma(\theta).
  \label{eq:finite-population-noise}
\end{equation}
Smaller batches have larger sampling variance; at $b=N$, this noise vanishes.
For two independent draws at these fixed parameters,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbb{E}[g_{t-1}^\top g_t]
    &=\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^2,\\
  \mathbb{E}\|g_t\|^2
    &=\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^2
      +\mathbb{E}\|\xi_t\|^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:frozen-noise-magnitudes}
\end{equation}
The shared full-gradient component contributes to the expected inner product,
while independent sampling fluctuations increase the expected squared gradient
norm without adding a persistent direction. Because cosine divides by random
gradient norms, its expectation is not the ratio of these two expectations. In
particular, adjacent sampled gradients can vary even when the parameters and
the full gradient are unchanged. Their projected change must therefore be
interpreted together with sampling noise, rather than attributed entirely to
curvature encountered through parameter motion.

\subsection{Normalized Displacement and Cumulative Interference}

Consecutive alignment characterizes one pair of steps. To describe how
directions accumulate over the complete trajectory, compare the sum of
gradient lengths with the length of their sum:
\begin{equation}
  A_T:=\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|,
  \qquad B_T:=\Bigl\|\sum_{t=1}^T g_t\Bigr\|.
  \label{eq:ab}
\end{equation}
For constant-step plain SGD, $\eta A_T$ is the discrete path length and
$\eta B_T=\|\Delta_T\|$ is the endpoint displacement. Provided $A_T>0$, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  R_T:=\frac{B_T}{A_T}
     &=\frac{\|\Delta_T\|}{\eta\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|},\\
  &\hspace{1em}0\leq R_T\leq1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:normalized-displacement}
\end{equation}
Values near one indicate that most of the accumulated path length remains as
net displacement. Smaller values indicate greater cumulative cancellation.
The ratio is relative to path length: a small $R_T$ does not by itself imply
small absolute displacement, poor loss reduction, or unstable training.
Together, $\rho_t$ and $R_T$ distinguish a local reversal of direction from
cancellation accumulated over a chosen horizon.

The fixed-parameter sampling calculation also clarifies this ratio. For
independent gradient draws at the same $\theta$, with
$0<\mathbb{E}\|g_t\|<\infty$, averaging the vectors preserves the full
gradient, whereas averaging their lengths retains sampling fluctuations.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{B_T}{A_T}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}
  \frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|}{\mathbb{E}\|g_t\|}.
  \label{eq:frozen-accumulation-limit}
\end{equation}
This describes gradient accumulation in a sampling control without parameter
updates. It shows how sampling can reduce the fraction of accumulated gradient
length that remains in the mean direction. Along an SGD trajectory, the
parameters and full-gradient direction also change, so the same fixed-state
calculation does not determine the observed $R_T$.

These diagnostics can be computed online without per-sample gradients,
Hessian eigenvectors, or a full stored gradient history. Consecutive cosine and the
projected change require the current and preceding gradients, their norms,
and one inner product. Accumulating $A_T$ and either $\sum_t g_t$ or the
parameter displacement gives $R_T$. The auxiliary storage is $O(p)$ and the
additional vector work is $O(p)$ per update, with no additional backward pass
or Hessian-vector product. Retaining parameter-sized vectors can still be
costly for a large model, but its cost does not grow with the training horizon.

Measurements must use the same parameter scope and loss reduction at every
update. We use the complete accumulated mini-batch gradient before clipping;
cosine is undefined when either gradient norm is zero, and $R_T$ is undefined
when $A_T=0$. Clipping, momentum, adaptive preconditioning, and learning-rate
schedules change the relationship between raw gradients and actual parameter
motion. Their gradient diagnostics remain descriptive, but the plain-SGD
displacement and curvature relations cannot be transferred to those updates
without accounting for the optimizer. The formulation therefore supplies a
practical geometric baseline for interpreting training trajectories alongside
loss and gradient-norm monitoring.

\subsection{Connection with Loss Decrease and Convergence}
\label{subsec:loss-connection}

Directional reinforcement and cancellation describe parameter motion. To
connect them with optimization progress, expand the loss along the same SGD
step. For the preceding update, from $\theta_{t-2}$ to $\theta_{t-1}$, the
second-order approximation gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-2})\\
&\quad\approx-\eta\|g_{t-1}\|^2
          \left(1-\frac{\eta\kappa_t}{2}\right)
          +\eta\xi_{t-1}^\top g_{t-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:loss-curvature}
\end{equation}
The same $\kappa_t$ that describes the projected gradient change therefore
also appears in the local loss change. For full-batch GD, the noise term
vanishes: the quadratic model predicts a loss decrease for $\eta\kappa_t<2$,
while the consecutive gradient turns against its predecessor for
$\eta\kappa_t>1$. Thus, $1<\eta\kappa_t<2$ can combine gradient reversal
with loss decrease. These statements are exact for a quadratic objective;
for a nonlinear loss, the omitted Taylor terms affect both predictions.
The condition concerns descent along the observed step, rather than
contraction of perturbations in every direction.

The role of consecutive cosine is also visible over two updates. Expanding
the loss along their combined displacement
$\theta_t-\theta_{t-2}=-\eta(g_{t-1}+g_t)$ yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-2})\\
&\quad\approx-\eta\left(\|g_{t-1}\|^2
                   +\|g_{t-1}\|\|g_t\|\rho_t\right)\\
&\qquad+\eta\xi_{t-1}^\top(g_{t-1}+g_t)\\
&\qquad+\frac{\eta^2}{2}(g_{t-1}+g_t)^\top
                    H_{t-1}(g_{t-1}+g_t).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:two_step_loss_interference}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_t$ enters the first-order projection of the combined step onto
the starting gradient. Curvature and noise also contribute, so its sign alone
does not determine whether the loss decreases. Appendix~\ref{appendix:two_step}
derives both relations and shows how the noise terms interact under unbiased
sampling.

These relations connect the diagnostics to loss progress without making
alignment a convergence criterion. Convergence requires control of loss
changes, noise, and approximation errors over the trajectory under suitable
assumptions. Positive cosine or a large $R_T$ does not supply that control;
negative cosine or strong cumulative cancellation can coexist with decreasing
loss. The geometric diagnostics should therefore be read alongside the loss
and the chosen convergence criterion.
