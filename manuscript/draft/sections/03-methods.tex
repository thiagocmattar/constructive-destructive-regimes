\section{The Signal-Interference Trade-off}

To account for the cumulative effects of the training trajectory in the stability analysis of SGD, we propose a decomposition of the parameter vector magnitude into interpretable terms that quantify both the energy of the updates and the alignment between gradients, measured both within individual batches and across successive batches.

In this sense, consider a neural network \( y = f(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \) with parameters \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d\). Let \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\) be the loss function defined over the training dataset
\(D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N,\)
where \(N\) is the total number of samples. The standard gradient descent update rule is
\[\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \; \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_t),\]
where \(\eta > 0\) is the learning rate, and \(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)\) is the gradient of the loss with respect to \(\theta\) at iteration \(t\). Let \(B_t \subset D\) be a mini-batch of size \(m = |B_t|\) at each iteration \(t\). The mini-batch gradient is defined as
\[G_{B_t}(\theta_t) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m g_i^{(t)},\]
where
\(g_i^{(t)} = \nabla\ell(\theta_t; x_i, y_i)\)
is the gradient of the loss for the \(i\)-th sample in batch \(B_t\) at iteration \(t\). The parameter update rule using the mini-batch gradient becomes
\[\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, G_{B_t}.\]
%
If the training dataset is uniformly distributed across mini-batches with $m$ samples, then one epoch consists of \(\frac{N}{m}\) iterations, covering the entire dataset once. Training for \(k\) epochs involves a total of \(T = k \times \frac{N}{m}\) iterations. After \(T\) iterations, the final parameter vector \(\theta_T\) is given by
\begin{equation}
\theta_T = \theta_0 - \eta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_{B_t}.
\label{eq:final_theta_expression}
\end{equation}
where \(\theta_0\) represents the parameters on initialization. Equation~\ref{eq:final_theta_expression} shows that the final parameter vector is obtained by accumulating gradient updates along the optimization trajectory. In deep learning, the magnitude and growth of the weight vector are closely related to the convergence behavior of the model \cite{lyu2020gradient,ji2020gradient}. This motivates analyzing the evolution of the parameter norm during training. Thus, taking the squared norm of \(\theta_T\), we can derive the following expression 

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\theta_T\|^2 
&= \Bigl\| \theta_0 - \eta \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_{B_t} \Bigr\|^2 \\
&= \|\theta_0\|^{2}
- 2\eta \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\theta_0^{\top} G_{B_t}
+ \eta^{2}
\Bigl\|\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_{B_t}\Bigr\|^{2}.
\label{eq:norm_split_terms}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This gives us three terms: (i) the norm of the parameters on initialization, (ii) the cross-alignment term between weight initialization and gradient trajectory, and (iii) the gradient trajectory. Let's call (ii) the \emph{initialization-trajectory alignment}
\begin{equation}
C
:=
-2\eta \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\theta_0^{\top} G_{B_t}.
\end{equation}
$C$ captures the alignment between the network initial parameters $\theta_0$ and the cumulative gradient trajectory. The last term \(\eta^{2} \Bigl\|\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_{B_t}\Bigr\|^{2}\) gives the norm contribution of the gradient over all iterations (trajectory). As \(G_{B_t} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} g_i^{(t)}\), this term can be expanded as follows

%Replacing \(G_{B_t}\) by \(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m g_i^{(t)},\) and expanding the cumulative gradient term gives

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Bigl\|\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m} g_i^{(t)}\Bigr\|^{2}
&=
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\sum_{u=0}^{T-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}
      g_i^{(t)\top} g_j^{(u)}
\\[6pt]
&=
\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
            \|g_i^{(t)}\|^{2}}_{\displaystyle S_1}
\;\; \\ 
&+\;
\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
            \sum_{\substack{j=1\\ j\neq i}}^{m}
            g_i^{(t)\top} g_j^{(t)}}_{\displaystyle S_2}
\;\;\\
&+\;
\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{u=0}^{t-1}
            \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}
            2\,g_i^{(t)\top} g_j^{(u)}}_{\displaystyle S_3}\; .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:s_def}
\end{equation}
%
The summation above lists every inner product between two sample–level gradients $g_i^{(t)}$ and $g_j^{(u)}$ collected during training.  We partition those index pairs into three sets:

\begin{itemize}
\item $S_1$ \textbf{(per–sample gradient energy)} gathers the diagonal terms where the two indices coincide $(t=u,\;i=j)$.  It therefore measures the total magnitude of every individual sample gradient over all iterations. 

\item $S_2$ \textbf{(intra‑batch alignment)} retains the pairs drawn \emph{within the same iteration} but from \emph{different} samples $(t=u,\; i\neq j)$. It therefore measures how gradients from different examples in the same mini-batch interact. It quantifies the degree of directional alignment between these gradients, reflecting the semantic similarity between samples. For instance, when a batch contains images with similar content or class labels, their corresponding gradients tend to align, resulting in a positive \(S_2\). This constructive alignment reinforces learning by increasing the effective step size. Conversely, a negative \(S_2\) indicates conflicting gradients—often arising from uncorrelated or contradictory samples—leading to partial signal cancellation within the batch.

\item $S_3$ \textbf{(cross‑step interference)} collects all gradient inner products across different training iterations $(t \neq u)$. It measures how gradients from earlier batches align or interfere with gradients from later ones. A large positive $S_3$ indicates that successive mini-batches produce correlated gradient directions, leading to a coherent trajectory in parameter space and more efficient accumulation of learning signal. This should occur when consecutive batches contain similar samples whose gradients reinforce each other. In contrast, when the content of consecutive batches is uncorrelated, gradient directions tend to decorrelate, causing $S_3$ to shrink. At the other extreme, $S_3 < 0$ implies destructive interference, in which updates from one batch partially undo progress made by earlier batches, leading to oscillatory or unstable learning dynamics. 

\end{itemize}
Together, we obtain the final norm decomposition
\begin{equation}
\boxed{%
\;
\|\theta_T\|^{2}
\;=\;
\|\theta_0\|^{2}
\;+\;
C
\;+\;
\frac{\eta^{2}}{m^{2}}\bigl(S_1 + S_2 + S_3\bigr).
\;}
\label{eq:thetaT_full}
\end{equation}
%
Equation ~\ref{eq:thetaT_full} explains the magnitude of the parameter vector at any training step, as the result of three conceptually distinct terms: (i) the baseline magnitude $\|\theta_0\|^{2}$ inherited from whatever initialization or pre‑training we choose; (ii) the alignment term $C$, which rewards or penalizes that initialization depending on how well it already points in the eventual descent direction; and (iii) the learning‑rate to batch-size scaled aggregate $(S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3})$, whose geometry tracks how stochastic gradients accumulate, through their individual strength, their coherence inside each batch, and their persistence across time.

\subsection{Practical Perspective}

While the decomposition in Eq.~\ref{eq:thetaT_full} is exact, it is not directly tractable in practice for large models. Computing all the trajectory terms requires storing and evaluating inner products between all sample-level gradients across all training steps, which is infeasible at scale. To obtain a practical diagnostic, we assume that gradient dependence is dominated by interactions between consecutive mini-batches. In particular, we approximate cross-step interactions as negligible beyond one iteration, i.e. \(G_{B_t}^{\top} G_{B_u} \approx 0\) for \(|t-u|>1\). With this simplification, $\|\theta_T\|^2$ can be approximated as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\theta_T\|^2
&\approx
\|\theta_0\|^2 
+ C \\
&+
\underbrace{\eta^2 \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \|G_{B_t}\|^2 }_{\displaystyle S_1 + S_2}
+ 
\underbrace{2\eta^2 \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} G_{B_{t-1}}^{\top} G_{B_{t}}}_{\displaystyle S_3},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:norm_with_correlation}
\end{equation}
The first term corresponds to the accumulated gradient energy and intra-batch alignment ($S_1+S_2$), while the second term captures cross-step interference ($S_3$). To make this interaction interpretable, we rewrite the inner product between consecutive gradients using cosine similarity,
\begin{equation}
\rho_t
=
\frac{G_{B_{t-1}}^{\top} G_{B_t}}
{\|G_{B_{t-1}}\|\,\|G_{B_t}\|}.
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Substituting back into Eq.~\ref{eq:norm_with_correlation} yields the approximation
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\theta_T\|^2
&\approx
\|\theta_0\|^2 
+ C \\
&+
\underbrace{\eta^2
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\|G_{B_t}\|^2}_{\displaystyle S_1 + S_2}
+
\underbrace{2\eta^2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\rho_t \,\|G_{B_{t-1}}\|\,\|G_{B_t}\|}_{\displaystyle S_3}
.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}
%
% We use $\rho_t$ to track trajectory coherence during training. When $\rho_t>0$, successive updates reinforce one another; when $\rho_t<0$, they partially cancel (overwriting); and when $\rho_t\approx 0$, updates are largely uncorrelated (noisy drift).

Although tracking consecutive-gradient alignment is not itself new \cite{xing2018walk}, our contribution is to place $\rho_t$ within the signal--interference decomposition and show that it directly governs the cross-step term $S_3$, thereby linking short-horizon gradient coherence to trajectory stability and loss reduction (Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_step_loss_interference}). In particular, \(\rho_t>0\) points to constructive accumulation of learning signal, \(\rho_t<0\) indicates destructive interference, and \(\rho_t\approx 0\) indicates random drift. Consistent with this picture, Figure~\ref{fig:traj_decomp} illustrates the trade-off: plain SGD operates in a destructive-interference regime (\(S_3/(S_1+S_2)<0\)), whereas momentum shifts it toward constructive accumulation (\(S_3/(S_1+S_2)>0\)), which is associated with lower loss; adding weight decay further improves robustness across a broader range of learning rates.

\subsection{Effects on Loss Dynamics}

% Utilizando a decomposição proposta, é possível avaliar os efeitos da interação entre os gradientes ao longo da trajetória na redução da função de custo.
The proposed framework provides insights into how gradient interactions affect the short-term evolution of the loss function. Consider two consecutive SGD steps $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta \, G_{B_t}$ and $\theta_{t+2}=\theta_{t+1}-\eta \, G_{B_{t+1}}$. Using the noise decomposition from Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd_noise_decomposition}, we write $G_{B_t} = g^{(t)} + \xi_t$, where $g^{(t)} := \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$ is the true gradient and $\xi_t$ is the zero-mean stochastic noise with $\mathrm{Cov}(\xi_t \mid \theta_t) \approx \frac{1}{m}\Sigma(\theta_t)$. Applying a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss at each step and a first-order expansion of the gradient $g^{(t+1)}$ around $\theta_t$ (see Appendix A), we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+2})
&\;\approx\;
\mathcal{L}(\theta_t) \\
&\;-\;\eta\Big(\|G_{B_t}\|^2 \;+\; \|G_{B_t}\|\,\|G_{B_{t+1}}\|\,\rho_{t+1}\Big) \\
&\;+\;\eta\,\xi_t^{\top}\!\bigl(G_{B_t} + G_{B_{t+1}}\bigr) \\
&\;+\;\eta^2\, G_{B_t}^{\top} H_t\, G_{B_{t+1}} \\
&\;+\;\frac{\eta^2}{2}\Big(
G_{B_t}^{\top} H_t\, G_{B_t}
\;+\;
G_{B_{t+1}}^{\top} H_{t+1}\, G_{B_{t+1}}
\Big),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:two_step_loss_interference}
\end{equation}
where \(H_t = \nabla^2\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)\), \(\rho_{t+1}\) is the cosine similarity between consecutive mini-batch gradients, and \(\xi_t = G_{B_t} - g^{(t)}\) is the sampling noise at step \(t\). A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:two_step}.

Analyzing the two terms proportional to \(\eta\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_step_loss_interference}, one can see three distinct elements that affect the two-step loss change: (i) per-step gradient energy \(\|G_{B_t}\|^2\), which captures the loss decrease from the current mini-batch update alone; (ii) cross-step coherence \(\|G_{B_t}\|\,\|G_{B_{t+1}}\|\,\rho_{t+1}\), which determines whether consecutive updates reinforce or partially cancel; and (iii) noise--trajectory coupling \(\xi_t^{\top}(G_{B_t}+G_{B_{t+1}})\), which measures how stochastic fluctuations project onto the combined update direction. Again, when \(\rho_{t+1} > 0\), consecutive updates point in similar directions, so the second step builds on the first and amplifies the loss decrease. When \(\rho_{t+1} < 0\), the second update opposes the first, so part of the progress made at step \(t\) is canceled at step \(t+1\). This may produce oscillatory behavior and dampen loss reduction across the two steps, even when local curvature measures still suggest stable training.

Furthermore, the noise--trajectory term 
\(\eta \,\xi_t^{\top}\!\bigl(G_{B_t} + G_{B_{t+1}}\bigr)\) 
reveals an opposing source to loss decrease.  To make it explicit, consider its interaction with the current mini-batch gradient:
\(\xi_t^{\top} G_{B_t} = \xi_t^{\top}g^{(t)} + \|\xi_t\|^2\).
The second term, \(\|\xi_t\|^2\), is always nonnegative and therefore contributes positively to the loss change regardless of the direction of the update. This means that even when consecutive gradients are well aligned ($\rho_t>0$) or nearly orthogonal ($\rho_t\approx0$), stochastic noise can still slow down convergence. We speculate here that \(\|\xi_t\|^2\) is aligned with the implicit-regularization argument of SGD, in which gradient noise can bias optimization away from purely deterministic descent and toward flatter, better-generalizing regions of the loss landscape~\cite{mandt2017sgd, jastrzebski2018three, smith2018bayesian}.

The remaining terms proportional to \(\eta^2\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_step_loss_interference} capture curvature effects. In particular, the cross-term \(G_{B_t}^{\top} H_t\, G_{B_{t+1}}\) shows that curvature influences how successive updates interact by weighting their shared directions through the local Hessian. Because this coupling depends on both batches, it cannot be inferred from \(\lambda_{\max}(H_t)\) or \(\Sigma(\theta_t)\) alone.

Equation~\ref{eq:two_step_loss_interference} also admits further refinement beyond the present analysis. In particular, it suggests a practical approach for noise diagnostics: if the true gradient \(g^{(t)}\) is approximated by a temporal average of mini-batch gradients with memory comparable to the number of batches, e.g. \(\hat{g}^{(t)} = (1-\beta)\,\hat{g}^{(t-1)} + \beta\,G_{B_t}\), where \(\beta\) is a memory coefficient, then the sampling noise can be estimated as \(\xi_t \approx G_{B_t} - \hat{g}^{(t)}\). This approximation enables tracking of all first-order (in \(\eta\)) contributions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:two_step_loss_interference} along the training trajectory. More broadly, the same expansion could be used to derive step-size conditions that explicitly incorporate cross-step coherence and noise--trajectory coupling, going beyond classical analyses based solely on curvature or worst-case eigenvalues. We leave these extensions for future work, as our focus here is on isolating and characterizing the signal--interference tradeoff.
