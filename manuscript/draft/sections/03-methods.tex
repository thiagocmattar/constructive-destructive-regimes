\section{Characterizing Constructive and Destructive Regimes}
\label{sec:displacement}

Loss spikes and oscillatory updates motivate practical diagnostics that can be
computed from gradients already available during training. We develop these
diagnostics by relating parameter displacement, gradient magnitudes and alignment, 
curvature and sampling noise. 
The proposed quantities characterize constructive and destructive regimes, 
and inform possible interventions without the additional overhead of computing Hessians.

Consider the plain SGD update in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd_map_stability}, with constant
learning rate $\eta>0$. As before, $\theta_t\in\mathbb{R}^p$ denotes the
parameters after update $t$, and $g_t$ is the mean mini-batch gradient evaluated
at $\theta_{t-1}$. Summing the updates gives the displacement from initialization,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_T:=\theta_T-\theta_0=-\eta\sum_{t=1}^T g_t.
  \label{eq:displacement}
\end{equation}
For a model trained from scratch, $\theta_0$ represents the initialized model that has not
yet learned from the training data. After $T$ updates, the trained parameters
$\theta_T$ encode what the model has learned within that training budget.
The displacement $\Delta_T$ captures the net parameter change produced
by training.
Expanding the squared displacement separates gradient magnitudes from the interactions 
between different steps 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \|\Delta_T\|^2
  ={}&\eta^2\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|^2\\
     &+2\eta^2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}g_u^\top g_t.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:magnitude-interaction}
\end{equation}
The terms $\|g_t\|^2$ are nonnegative and determine the squared lengths of
individual SGD steps through $\eta^2\|g_t\|^2$. In full-batch GD, a larger
gradient norm indicates a steeper local loss and, therefore, a larger first-order decrease
along the negative gradient. The cross-step terms
$g_u^\top g_t$ describe how these steps interact -- positive inner products
reinforce the displacement, whereas negative inner products cancel
part of it. Large gradient norms can therefore coexist with cancellation along the training trajectory, and
destructive interference (i.e. persistent $g_u^\top g_t < 0$) can limit displacement growth.

To isolate directional alignment from gradient magnitudes, define the cosine
similarity between any two nonzero gradients and its consecutive-step form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \rho_{u,t}&:=\frac{g_u^\top g_t}{\|g_u\|\|g_t\|},
       &&1\leq u<t\leq T.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
In this setup, the alignment between consecutive gradients is
$\rho_t:=\rho_{t-1,t}$, $t=2,\ldots,T$.
Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:magnitude-interaction}
expresses the squared displacement entirely through gradient norms and
directional alignment
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\Delta_T\|^2={}&\eta^2\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|^2\\
 &+2\eta^2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}
    \|g_u\|\|g_t\|\rho_{u,t}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:magnitude-alignment}
\end{equation}
For fixed gradient norms, positive $\rho_{u,t}$ increases the squared net
displacement, negative $\rho_{u,t}$ contributes cancellation, and orthogonal
gradients have no pairwise contribution. In practice, 
$\rho_t$ provides an online diagnostic of the
interaction between successive updates: persistent positive or negative
values characterize constructive or destructive regimes.

\subsection{Directional Curvature and Consecutive Alignment}

For mini-batch SGD, we explicitly incorporate stochastic gradient noise in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd_noise_decomposition},
$g_t=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1})+\xi_t$.
Suppose the full objective $\mathcal{L}$ is twice continuously differentiable
along the preceding step, and write
$H_{t-1}:=\nabla^2\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-2})$.
Expanding the full gradient around $\theta_{t-2}$ and substituting the preceding
SGD update yields
\begin{equation}
  g_t=(I-\eta H_{t-1})g_{t-1}+(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})+r_t,
  \label{eq:gradient-evolution}
\end{equation}
where $r_t$ is the Taylor remainder. Neglecting $r_t$ recovers the first-order
approximation. Taking the inner product with $g_{t-1}$ then gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|g_{t-1}\|\|g_t\|\rho_t
 &\approx\underbrace{\|g_{t-1}\|^2}_{\text{Gradient magnitude}}\\
 &\quad-\underbrace{\eta g_{t-1}^{\top}H_{t-1}g_{t-1}}_{\text{Curvature contribution}}\\
 &\quad+\underbrace{g_{t-1}^{\top}(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})}_{\text{Noise contribution}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rho-expanded}
\end{equation}
Positive curvature reduces the component of the next gradient along the
preceding direction. Its strength relative to the preceding squared norm
is the directional curvature
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top H_{t-1}g_{t-1}}{\|g_{t-1}\|^2},
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
Thus, $\kappa_t$ measures how strongly a step changes the gradient along
the direction actually followed, whereas $\rho_t$ measures the resulting
alignment. Sampling modifies this response through the normalized noise
interaction
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_t:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})}
                            {\|g_{t-1}\|^2}.
  \label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
Dividing Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho-expanded} by the two gradient norms gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_t\approx\frac{\|g_{t-1}\|}{\|g_t\|}
                   (1-\eta\kappa_t+\varepsilon_t).
  \label{eq:rho-normalized}
\end{equation}
In full-batch GD, $\varepsilon_t=0$. Within this first-order approximation,
$\eta\kappa_t<1$ retains a positive projection, while $\eta\kappa_t>1$
reverses it: the next update partially opposes the preceding step.
At $\eta\kappa_t=1$, the new gradient has lost its component along the
old direction. It is orthogonal if nonzero; if it vanishes, cosine is
undefined. The norm ratio determines how this projected change translates
into the magnitude of $\rho_t$.

The threshold at one also has an optimization interpretation. Along the
full-gradient direction, the local quadratic loss model is
$\mathcal L(\theta_{t-2}-\alpha g_{t-1})\approx
\mathcal L(\theta_{t-2})-\alpha\|g_{t-1}\|^2+
\alpha^2\kappa_t\|g_{t-1}\|^2/2$.
For $\kappa_t>0$, its directional minimum is at $\alpha=1/\kappa_t$.
Negative alignment therefore indicates that the step has passed this
directional minimum under the approximation. Other gradient components
may remain: this threshold identifies directional overshoot, rather than
a stationary point or the loss-increase boundary derived below.

This interpretation leads to a measurable diagnostic. Rearranging
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho-normalized} gives
\begin{equation}
  \eta\widehat\kappa_t
  :=1-\rho_t\frac{\|g_t\|}{\|g_{t-1}\|}
  \approx \eta\kappa_t-\varepsilon_t.
  \label{eq:kappa-estimate}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side uses only consecutive gradients: it measures their
finite-step projected change without a Hessian evaluation. In full GD it
estimates directional curvature when the Taylor remainder is small; in
SGD it also contains sampling noise. It characterizes the realized step,
whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:quad_stability_condition} concerns perturbations
along the most curved local direction.


\subsection{Noise Contribution under Mini-Batch Sampling}
\label{subsec:sampling-noise}

Unbiased sampling does not make the noise interaction vanish. Write
$g_{t-1}=\nabla\mathcal L(\theta_{t-2})+\xi_{t-1}$.
Fresh, conditionally unbiased batches give
$\mathbb E[g_{t-1}^{\top}\xi_t]=0$ and
$\mathbb E[\nabla\mathcal L(\theta_{t-2})^{\top}\xi_{t-1}]=0$.
With finite second moments, the preceding gradient contributes its own noise energy:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})]
  =-\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{t-1}\|^2]\leq0.
  \label{eq:noise-expectation}
\end{equation}
Thus, even unbiased sampling noise contributes a systematic
nonpositive term to the expected unnormalized interaction
between consecutive gradients.

This contribution is directly related to mini-batch size.
Consider a mini-batch of $b$ examples sampled uniformly
without replacement from $N>1$ training examples.
Using the per-sample gradient covariance $\Sigma(\theta)$
defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad_cov_def}, the sampling
variance satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{t-1}\|^2]
  =\frac{N-b}{b(N-1)}
    \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}\Sigma(\theta_{t-2})].
  \label{eq:finite-population-noise}
\end{equation}
At fixed parameters, smaller batches produce larger sampling
variance and, consequently, a larger negative expected noise
contribution. For full-batch gradients ($b=N$), this contribution
vanishes.

Noise can weaken directional coherence by enlarging gradient norms
relative to their shared signal. For independent estimates at two fixed
states, let $\mu,\nu$ be the nonzero full gradients and $v_\mu,v_\nu$
the expected squared noise norms. When sampled norms concentrate,
\[
\mathbb E[\cos(\mu+\xi,\nu+\zeta)]\approx
\frac{\cos(\mu,\nu)}
{\sqrt{(1+v_\mu/\|\mu\|^2)(1+v_\nu/\|\nu\|^2)}}.
\]
This attenuation moves either sign toward zero. At the same fixed state,
the common full-gradient direction gives the special case
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[\rho_t]\approx
  \frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^2}
  {\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^2+
  \frac{N-b}{b(N-1)}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma(\theta)}.
  \label{eq:noise-cosine-approx}
\end{equation}
Here the expected cosine is nonnegative; the approximation quantifies
the dilution of a common direction by sampling. During SGD, a sampled
update also changes the next state, coupling noise and curvature.
The fixed-state attenuation argument therefore isolates a mechanism,
while the batch-size intervention in Section~\ref{sec:sgd-noise-experiments}
tests its expression along evolving trajectories.


\subsection{Normalized Displacement and Cumulative Interference}
\label{subsec:normalized-displacement}

While the consecutive cosine similarity $\rho_t$ measures local lag-1
directional alignment, it does not capture interactions between
more distant updates (lag$\geq2$). To measure their cumulative effect,
we define the normalized displacement
\begin{equation}
  R_T:=
  \frac{\|\Delta_T\|}
       {\eta\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|}
  =\frac{\|\sum_{t=1}^T g_t\|}
        {\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|},
  \qquad 0\leq R_T\leq1,
  \label{eq:normalized-displacement}
\end{equation}
For constant-step plain
SGD, the denominator represents the total distance traveled
in parameter space, while the numerator measures the net
displacement from initialization. Thus, $R_T$ quantifies the
fraction of accumulated step length retained as net displacement.

Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:magnitude-alignment}, this ratio satisfies
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_T^2
=\frac{
\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|^2
+2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}
\|g_u\|\|g_t\|\rho_{u,t}
}{
\left(\sum_{t=1}^T\|g_t\|\right)^2
}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cumulative-interference}
\end{equation}
Unlike $\rho_t$, which considers only adjacent gradients,
$R_T$ incorporates interactions across all temporal lags.
When $R_T$ is near one, almost all traveled distance survives as net
displacement; when it is near zero, little does. Repeated back-and-forth
steps and weakly coherent sampled directions both suggest low retention.
For example, equal-length orthogonal updates give $R_T=1/\sqrt T$,
whereas equal opposing pairs cancel exactly. We therefore expect small
$R_T$ when such behavior persists over a substantial part of an SGD path.
Its value is relative to traveled distance: absolute displacement can
still grow, and the sign of the complete interaction in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cumulative-interference} requires the full decomposition.

\subsection{Directional Overshoot and Loss Increase}
\label{subsec:loss-connection}

Directional overshoot need not undo the loss decrease already made along
a step. Define its full-objective change as
$\delta\mathcal L_{t-1}:=\mathcal L(\theta_{t-1})-
\mathcal L(\theta_{t-2})$.
The second-order approximation derived in Appendix~\ref{appendix:loss-curvature}
separates three contributions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\delta\mathcal L_{t-1}
&\approx\underbrace{-\frac{\eta}{2}\|g_{t-1}\|^2}_{\text{Gradient magnitude}}\\
&\quad\underbrace{-\frac{\eta}{2}\rho_t\|g_{t-1}\|\|g_t\|}_{\text{Directional interference}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\frac{\eta}{2}g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_{t-1}+\xi_t)}_{\text{Noise interaction}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:loss-curvature}
\end{equation}
The magnitude term favors descent. The interference term reinforces it
for positive alignment and opposes it for negative alignment: destructive
interference contributes positively to the predicted loss change.
With negligible noise and nonzero gradients, the approximation predicts
a loss increase precisely when
\begin{equation}
  \rho_t<-\frac{\|g_{t-1}\|}{\|g_t\|}.
  \label{eq:loss-rise-alignment}
\end{equation}
This requires both negative alignment and gradient-norm growth. Neither
alone suffices: the opposing component of the new gradient must exceed
the preceding gradient magnitude. Large norms can therefore accompany
descent, just as negative cosine can accompany a smaller loss decrease.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho-normalized} with zero noise gives
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mathcal L_{t-1}\approx
  \frac{\eta}{2}\|g_{t-1}\|^2(\eta\kappa_t-2).
  \label{eq:loss-rise-curvature}
\end{equation}
The two thresholds distinguish overshooting the directional minimum
($\eta\kappa_t>1$) from overshooting far enough to raise the loss
($\eta\kappa_t>2$). Between them, the gradients oppose each other while
the step still improves the objective. These are local quadratic
predictions; finite-step nonlinear effects and the explicit SGD noise
term can change the observed sign. A transient increase becomes a loss
peak only if subsequent updates recover.
