\section{Introduction}

Neural networks often reduce training loss while successive gradients point
in nearly opposite directions. This behavior becomes especially visible at
large learning rates, where rapid overall progress can coexist with oscillation
and sharp loss spikes~\cite{cohen2021edge, chowdhery2022palm, olmo2025furious}.
For practitioners, the important question is when such oscillation remains
productive and when it develops into a damaging excursion. Learning-rate
schedules, momentum and clipping help manage this behavior. Explaining their
effect requires an observable connection between the traversed curvature,
successive gradient interactions and loss.

We approach this question through \emph{constructive and destructive regimes}.
The cosine similarity $\rho_t$ between adjacent raw gradients identifies
the regime: positive values indicate directional reinforcement, and negative
values indicate directional opposition. The gradient norm supplies the
complementary information about the strength of that interaction. Together,
they distinguish persistent reversal during descent from reversal accompanied
by rapid gradient amplification. This distinction is central to the paper:
a destructive regime can sustain loss reduction, while sufficiently strong
amplification can turn its local oscillations into loss-increasing steps.
We make this transition accessible through a directional-curvature estimate
computed from gradients already produced during training.

Classical stability analysis relates step size to the Hessian spectrum, and
stochastic extensions incorporate variation induced by
sampling~\cite{polyak1964methods, jastrzebski2020break}. The Edge-of-Stability
literature establishes that deep networks can continue to improve while
operating near or beyond the local quadratic stability
boundary~\cite{cohen2021edge, arora2022understanding, damian2023self}.
Gradient-product diagnostics and hypergradient methods already exploit
successive directions to detect optimization phases or adapt the learning
rate~\cite{chee2018diagnostics, pesme2020diagnostics, baydin2018hypergradient}.
Our contribution connects these temporal interactions to curvature estimation,
regime transitions, net parameter motion and the anatomy of loss spikes.

The central measurement is $\eta\widehat\kappa_t$, which combines adjacent
cosine with the gradient-norm ratio. Under full-batch GD, dividing by the
learning rate gives the directional curvature averaged along the completed
step, with a controlled approximation to the starting-point curvature.
Retaining the preceding gradient makes this estimate available at every
ordinary backward pass using $O(p)$ vector operations for $p$ parameters.
Neither a Hessian nor a Hessian--vector product is evaluated. Local loss
expansions connect the estimate to destructive amplification, while an exact
SGD displacement decomposition and normalized displacement describe how
these interactions accumulate over the trajectory.

We first characterize the regimes in controlled full-batch GD and mini-batch
SGD experiments on CIFAR-10 and WikiText-2. We then extend the analysis to
Adam, whose adaptive directions transform the raw gradient before each
update~\cite{kingma2014adam}. Raw-gradient diagnostics still expose the loss
landscape encountered along that adaptive path: the largest spikes show
diagnostic changes before the loss maximum, and the Transformer example
reveals a warning before its pronounced rise. Across 129 recovered spikes
larger than 10\%, gradient norms grow in every event and 117 of the 127 defined
pre-rise cosines identify a destructive regime. Combining that initial regime
with subsequent norm amplification accounts for a substantial fraction of
the variation in spike magnitude.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
 \item An online directional-curvature estimate from consecutive gradients,
 with a formal path-average interpretation, explicit sampling-noise terms,
 and one-gradient storage. It connects constructive/destructive regimes to
 local loss changes and cumulative parameter displacement.
 \item A controlled empirical characterization showing how destructive regimes
 sustain descent, reduce displacement retention and change under mini-batch
 sampling.
 \item An extension to Adam that identifies early signs and recurring anatomy
 of loss spikes through raw-gradient alignment and amplification, supported by
 individual events, amplitude distributions and quantitative associations.
\end{itemize}
