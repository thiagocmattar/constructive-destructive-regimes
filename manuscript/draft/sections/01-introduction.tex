\section{Introduction}

Gradient-based training can make progress while repeatedly reversing direction.
Successive updates may nearly cancel, gradient norms may grow, and the loss may
oscillate, yet the objective decreases over a longer window. Conversely, a
smoother trajectory need not reach a lower loss at the same training budget.
These behaviors complicate the interpretation of the signals used to monitor
training and to motivate changes in learning rate, batch size or clipping.
As training budgets grow~\cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022chinchilla},
understanding what those signals measure becomes increasingly useful: an
intervention can change the appearance of a trajectory without improving its
outcome.

Classical analyses connect learning rate and curvature to local contraction
near a minimum~\cite{polyak1964methods,bertsekas1997nonlinear}. The
Edge-of-Stability literature establishes that neural networks can continue
descending while oscillating near or beyond the corresponding fixed-quadratic
threshold~\cite{cohen2021edge,arora2022understanding,damian2023self}.
Consecutive-gradient products also have an established role in convergence
diagnostics and learning-rate adaptation~\cite{chee2018diagnostics,
pesme2020diagnostics,baydin2018hypergradient}, and gradient differences provide
classical secant information~\cite{barzilai1988two}. Thus neither gradient
alignment nor oscillatory descent is a new observation. The question we address
is how to interpret these measurements together along a finite training path:
\emph{where does cancellation occur, how does the remaining motion accumulate,
and what does either observation establish about loss progress?}

We develop a diagnostic framework around \emph{constructive} and
\emph{destructive} gradient interaction. Positive inner products reinforce
parameter displacement; negative products cancel a component of it. These
terms describe geometry at a specified temporal scale. In particular,
destructive adjacent interaction can coexist with constructive interaction
across the complete path. An exact displacement decomposition retains all
temporal lags, while pairing adjacent updates separates immediate cancellation
from the accumulation of the residual motion. A reference with zero total
cross-step interaction makes the magnitude of that accumulation interpretable.
Together, these quantities locate cancellation more precisely than either an
adjacent cosine or a path-length ratio alone.

The link to the objective depends on the update rule. For plain gradient
descent (GD), consecutive gradients give a directional secant, whose
path-average curvature interpretation is exact under smoothness. A local
quadratic model then distinguishes reversal from a loss-increasing step using
both the angle and the norm ratio. Under stochastic gradient descent (SGD),
sampling changes the observed gradient and the subsequently visited state.
Under Adam, moment averaging and coordinate scaling additionally separate the
raw gradient from the applied update. We make these distinctions explicit so
that the same measured cosine is not assigned incompatible meanings across
optimizers.

The experiments show why these distinctions matter. All nine controlled GD
trajectories have positive total interaction, although seven have negative
adjacent interaction. Matched SGD continuations give similar mean cosines
with opposite loss changes. Under Adam, all 794 analyzed rise-phase steps
point downhill to first order despite a net rise in loss. Existing Pythia
runs and matched interventions then test how changes in these observations
relate to a specified objective outcome and time horizon.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
 \item A joint account of local cancellation and cumulative parameter motion,
 using an all-lag displacement decomposition, a zero-cross-term reference and
 an exact factorization into within-pair cancellation and residual accumulation.
 \item An optimizer-aware interpretation of these measurements, connecting the
 classical directional secant to curvature, sampling and finite-step loss
 changes, and distinguishing raw-gradient response from Adam's actual motion.
 \item An empirical characterization across image classification and language
 modeling that establishes the practical consequences of these distinctions:
 reversal can accompany progress, raw-gradient reversal need not imply an
 uphill update, and improved alignment need not imply improved loss.
\end{itemize}
The contribution is the combined diagnostic account and its empirical tests.
We do not propose a new optimizer or a general stability criterion. This scope
supports the design and assessment of future interventions without assuming
that suppressing destructive alignment is itself a desirable objective.
