\section{Introduction}

Neural scaling laws have ushered in new paradigms for interpreting deep learning dynamics. By establishing that performance gains follow predictable behavior relative to model size, dataset scale, and compute, stochastic optimization methods have been pushed toward increasingly extended training horizons while operating under tight efficiency constraints~\cite{hestness2017deep, kaplan2020scaling, henighan2020scaling, hoffmann2022chinchilla}. In this regime, optimization stability becomes an important practical requirement, since unstable training may result in slow convergence and high sensitivity to hyperparameter choices. In practice, stability is often maintained through a set of heuristics, such as momentum, batch-size scaling, normalization, and learning-rate schedules~\cite{goyal2017accurate, smith2017cyclical, loshchilov2016sgdr, ioffe2015batch, krogh1991simple}. Yet, despite their effectiveness, it is not fully understood why these mechanisms work, and under what conditions they fail.
% Yet, despite their effectiveness, there is still no fully satisfactory account of why these mechanisms help to stabilize the optimization trajectory, and under what conditions they fail.

Classical local analyses relate the permissible step size to curvature in
sharp directions of the loss~\cite{polyak1964methods, bertsekas1997nonlinear}.
Stochastic extensions, including the break-even point framework, also account
for curvature variation induced by sampling~\cite{jastrzebski2020break}.
The Edge-of-Stability literature shows that deep networks can continue to make
progress while oscillating near, or beyond, the nominal sharpness
threshold~\cite{cohen2021edge, arora2022understanding, damian2023self}.
These results motivate a complementary diagnostic question: how do the
magnitudes and directions of successive gradients combine into parameter
motion along the observed training trajectory? Gradient information already
computed during optimization makes this question accessible without estimating
the full Hessian spectrum or gradient-noise covariance at every update.

To examine these interactions, we describe parameter motion through gradient
magnitudes and directional alignment. Starting from an untrained reference,
training changes the parameters through which the model expresses what it has
learned. Displacement provides a geometric description of that change; it does
not quantify retained knowledge or establish whether the change improves the
objective. Positive and negative gradient interactions respectively reinforce
and cancel components of the accumulated motion. Their effect must be evaluated
at the relevant temporal horizon and distinguished from loss reduction.

We develop an exact finite-time decomposition of SGD displacement that retains
all cross-step interactions. Consecutive-gradient cosine supplies a local online
diagnostic, while normalized displacement measures the fraction of accumulated
step length retained as net motion. Local expansions relate gradient changes
to directional curvature and sampling noise. The distinction between these
quantities is consequential: a trajectory can reverse successive gradient
directions while continuing to reduce loss, and sampling can change both the
measured alignment and the subsequent path.

The same distinction becomes essential under adaptive optimization. Adam
already computes raw gradients and moment estimates~\cite{kingma2014adam};
its parameter updates combine temporal averaging, coordinate scaling and the
learning rate. Monitoring these successive quantities can reveal changes
that a single alignment statistic would obscure. We use language-model
measurements and schedule interventions to illustrate their interpretation,
while separating descriptive geometric responses from the development of
an improved learning-rate rule.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A displacement-based account of local and cumulative gradient
    interaction, with explicit connections to curvature, sampling noise and
    finite-step loss changes;
    \item Controlled full-batch and mini-batch experiments that distinguish
    directional reversal, displacement retention and objective progress;
    \item A practical extension to Adam that separates raw-gradient information,
    momentum, adaptive directions and actual parameter motion, supported by
    language-model measurements and matched schedule interventions.
\end{itemize}
