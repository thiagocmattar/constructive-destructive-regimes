\section{Background: Stability Analysis of SGD}

Intuitively, stable optimization occurs when updates reinforce each other and gradually shift parameters toward a point of minimum loss. Instability therefore arises when updates overshoot, oscillate, or partially undo the progress made by previous steps~\cite{polyak1964methods, bertsekas1997nonlinear}. Understanding this behavior requires examining the mechanisms that govern how updates evolve from one iteration to the next. Two factors have traditionally played a central role in explaining these dynamics: the \textit{local curvature} of the loss landscape and the \textit{stochasticity} introduced by mini-batch sampling.

\subsection{Local Curvature of the Loss Function and Stability}

Training can be interpreted as a discrete-time dynamical system in which the local curvature of the loss function determines the stability of the updates~\cite{polyak1964methods, bertsekas1997nonlinear}. The curvature indicates how sensitive the loss is to parameter changes in different directions: when curvature is small, updates tend to produce smooth descent toward a minimum, whereas in directions of high curvature the same step can overshoot the minimum and induce oscillatory behavior~\cite{polyak1964methods, bertsekas1997nonlinear, foret2021sharpness}. For this reason, classical analyses of gradient descent relate stability to the spectrum of the Hessian of the loss function, whose eigenvalues quantify directional curvature and indicate how sensitive optimization is to the choice of learning rate~\cite{polyak1964methods, bertsekas1997nonlinear}.

Under this view, let $\theta_t\in\mathbb{R}^p$ denote the parameters after update $t$, and let $g_t$ denote a mini-batch gradient evaluated at $\theta_{t-1}$. For a constant learning rate $\eta>0$, the SGD update is
\begin{equation}
\theta_t
= 
\theta_{t-1} - \eta \, g_t.
\label{eq:sgd_map_stability}
\end{equation}
For full-batch GD, $g_t=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1})$, where $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the full training objective. Defining $H_t:=\nabla^2\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1})$, the Jacobian of the deterministic update map is
\begin{equation}
J_t
=
I - \eta\, H_t.
\label{eq:jacobian_sgd}
\end{equation}
Consider a small perturbation \(e_{t-1}\) around $\theta_{t-1}$. Under the local linearization of the full-batch GD update, this perturbation evolves as
\[
e_t \approx J_t e_{t-1} = (I-\eta H_t)e_{t-1},
\]
In a locally quadratic regime near a minimum, the Hessian is approximately constant and admits eigenpairs \((\lambda_i,v_i)\). Decomposing the perturbation as \(e_t=\sum_i \delta_t^{(i)} v_i\), each eigendirection evolves independently according to
\[
\delta_t^{(i)}=(1-\eta\lambda_i)\,\delta_{t-1}^{(i)}.
\]
Thus, the factor \(1-\eta\lambda_i\) determines whether perturbations along direction \(v_i\) decay or grow. A classical necessary and sufficient condition for linear stability is
\begin{equation}
\max_i |1-\eta\lambda_i| < 1
\; \Leftrightarrow \;
\eta\,\lambda_{\max}(H_t) < 2,
\label{eq:quad_stability_condition}
\end{equation}
which means that all perturbation components contract over time~\cite{polyak1964methods, bertsekas1997nonlinear}. In other words, if \(0<\eta\lambda_i<1\), the perturbation decays monotonically along \(v_i\); if \(1<\eta\lambda_i<2\), it alternates sign while shrinking, producing oscillations around the minimum; and if \(\eta\lambda_i>2\), it alternates sign while growing, leading to divergence. In over-parameterized networks, however, the Hessian spectrum is typically highly anisotropic, with a small number of outlier directions carrying most of the sharpness, while the bulk remains near zero~\cite{sagun2017hessian, ghorbani2019density}. Although this suggests monitoring \(\lambda_{\max}(H_{t})\) as a stability diagnostic, estimating leading Hessian eigenvalues online in large neural networks is computationally expensive, and mini-batch estimates provide only noisy, pointwise proxies of the local geometry.


\subsection{Mini-Batch Stochasticity}

As a result, consecutive mini-batches can induce different update directions, causing the optimization trajectory to fluctuate from one step to the next~\cite{jastrzebski2018three, smith2018bayesian}. The magnitude and structure of this stochasticity depend on the batch size, and they interact with the local geometry of the loss landscape to shape the overall training dynamics~\cite{mandt2017sgd, hu2019diffusion, jastrzebski2018three}. A standard way to formalize this effect is through the covariance of mini-batch gradient noise around the full gradient. However, this quantity is difficult to estimate in practice, since computing it exactly requires access to the full gradient at each optimization step.

% Stochastic optimization adds a second geometric object: the noise covariance. 
A complementary line of work studies SGD through the lens of stochastic optimization, interpreting the mini-batch gradient as a noisy approximation of the full gradient computed over the entire dataset \cite{mandt2017sgd, hu2019diffusion, mandt2017stochastic, jastrzebski2018three}. In this sense, each SGD step combines a deterministic descent component with a zero-mean random fluctuation induced by sampling, which covariance provides information about how noise is distributed across parameter-space directions and how it may affect trajectory stability. Formally, under fresh, conditionally unbiased sampling, we write the mini-batch gradient as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g_t
&= \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1}) + \xi_t,\\
\mathbb{E}[\xi_t\mid \theta_{t-1}]
&= 0,\\
\mathrm{Cov}(\xi_t\mid \theta_{t-1})
&\approx \frac{1}{b}\,\Sigma(\theta_{t-1}),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sgd_noise_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where \(\xi_t\) is the sampling noise, $b$ is the batch size, and \(\Sigma(\theta_{t-1})\) denotes the covariance matrix of per-sample gradients around the full gradient. At the current parameters, write \(h_i:=\nabla\ell_i(\theta_{t-1})\), where $\ell_i$ is the loss for sample $i$, and \(\mu:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N h_i=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t-1})\), where $N$ is the dataset size. The covariance matrix is
\begin{equation}
\Sigma(\theta_{t-1})
:=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl(h_i-\mu\bigr)\bigl(h_i-\mu\bigr)^{\top},
\label{eq:grad_cov_def}
\end{equation}
and the scalar dispersion of per-sample gradients around the full gradient is given by the trace of \(\Sigma(\theta_{t-1})\)
\[
\mathrm{tr} (\Sigma(\theta_{t-1}))=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|h_i-\mu\|^2.
\]
This makes clear why $\Sigma(\theta_{t-1})$ is generally not a viable online diagnostic: computing it requires access to the full gradient $\mu$ and all per-sample gradients $\{h_i\}_{i=1}^N$ at each training step, which is impractical and incurs at least a full-pass cost per step.

\subsection{Curvature--Noise Interaction}

% Within this curvature--noise viewpoint, two influential trajectory analysis highlight what can (and cannot) be explained by pointwise stability criteria: the \emph{break-even point} for SGD~\cite{jastrzebski2020break} and the \emph{edge-of-stability} (EoS) phenomenon for GD~\cite{cohen2021edge, arora2022understanding, damian2023self}. Both discuss stability along the sharpest direction, but they expose different limitations of purely sharpness-based reasoning.

The \emph{break-even point} framework of Jastrzebski et al.~\cite{jastrzebski2020break} is an attempt to extend the classical curvature-based stability condition to SGD. The central idea is to analyze stability along the sharpest direction of the loss landscape while explicitly accounting for mini-batch stochasticity. More precisely, the framework proposes a one-dimensional necessary-and-sufficient condition for SGD stability at iteration \(t\),
\begin{equation}
(1 - \eta \lambda_{\max}(H_t))^2 + s_t^2 \frac{\eta^2(N-b)}{b(N-1)} \;\le\; 1,
\label{eq:break_even_condition}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_{\max}(H_t)\) is the top Hessian eigenvalue, \(N\) is the dataset size, \(b\) is the batch size, and \(s_t^2\) denotes the variance across training examples of the curvature measured along the leading Hessian direction. Large \(s_t^2\) means different mini-batches see substantially different local curvature, while small \(s_t^2\) means they see similar curvature. In this sense, Eq.~\eqref{eq:break_even_condition} can be read as a stochastic extension of the deterministic stability condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:quad_stability_condition}, so that the first term captures curvature-induced amplification, while the second captures the destabilizing effect of mini-batch stochasticity.

Under this definition, the break-even point is defined as the first iteration at which the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:break_even_condition} reaches \(1\). Intuitively, it marks the end of an early phase during which the choice of learning rate and batch size can strongly shape the later optimization trajectory. In this sense, a central conjecture of this framework is that, prior to this point, hyperparameters largely determine the maximum curvature and stochasticity levels encountered later in training, thereby setting the regime in which the subsequent dynamics unfold.

Although the local analysis simplifies both the interpretation and the mathematical treatment of the stability problem, it is subject to well-known limitations. For instance, the Edge-of-Stability (EoS) phenomenon challenges the local-quadratic picture even in deterministic gradient descent (GD). Across architectures and tasks, full-batch GD often exhibits progressive sharpening, with the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, $\lambda_{\max}(H_t)$, rising until it hovers near (and sometimes above) the nominal threshold $2/\eta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:quad_stability_condition}), while optimization continues to make progress despite short-term non-monotonicity~\cite{cohen2021edge, arora2022understanding, damian2023self}. Thus, successful training can coexist with local linear instability for extended periods, indicating that sharpness alone does not fully explain and determine trajectory-level behavior.

Together, these results clarify both the value and the limitation of curvature--noise interaction criteria. Quantities such as the leading Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}(H_t)$ and the gradient-noise covariance $\Sigma(\theta_{t-1})$ characterize local geometry and stochasticity, but can be expensive to estimate online. Their pointwise values also leave open how successive updates interact and accumulate over a training trajectory. This motivates complementary diagnostics that describe directional reinforcement and cancellation using quantities available during optimization.

\subsection{Practical Stability Analysis in Large-Model Pretraining}
\label{subsec:practical-stability}

Large-model training reports approach stability through observable symptoms and
interventions on the training recipe. An instructive example is PaLM:
Section~5.1 reports approximately 20 loss spikes during training of the
540B model despite global gradient clipping. The authors recovered by returning
to a checkpoint about 100 steps before a spike and skipping 200--500 batches.
Replaying those batches from a different parameter state did not reproduce the
spikes, suggesting an interaction between data and model state rather than a
property of the batches alone~\cite{chowdhery2022palm}.

Reports accompanying released model weights provide further operational
evidence. During OPT-175B training, loss divergence was associated with failures
of the dynamic loss scale and growing final-layer activation norms. Recovery
involved reducing the learning rate and restarting from an earlier checkpoint;
lowering the gradient-clipping threshold from 1.0 to 0.3 also improved stability
early in training~\cite{zhang2022opt}. These observations motivate monitoring
the numerical state of training alongside the loss.

OLMo~2 makes this diagnostic process explicit by measuring a rolling spike
score on training loss and gradient norm, and using controlled ablations to
study initialization, normalization, and optimizer settings. Its revised recipe
combines global gradient clipping with changes including query--key
normalization, reordered normalization, and a logit regularizer
($z$-loss)~\cite{olmo2025furious}. In Llama~3, the authors report a stable
pretraining recipe with few loss spikes and no interventions for divergence;
they use learning-rate warmup and a staged batch-size schedule, and emphasize
FP32 gradient accumulation and reduction for numerical
stability~\cite{grattafiori2024llama3}. These reports document both failure
recovery and recipes that avoid recurrent failures, rather than a universal
instability mechanism.

Loss, gradient norm, and activation statistics are practical monitoring signals,
but gradient magnitude alone does not describe the relationship between
successive directions. A large gradient can reinforce or oppose its predecessor,
and a sequence of individually bounded steps can still exhibit cumulative
cancellation. We therefore seek a complementary description that can be
computed from training gradients and interpreted through their temporal
geometry. The following section develops parameter displacement and
consecutive-gradient cosine similarity for this purpose, connecting the latter
to directional curvature and gradient noise without requiring online Hessian
eigendecomposition.
