\section{Related Work and Conceptual Foundations}
\label{sec:background}

% We review why oscillating updates can accompany loss reduction, what gradients reveal about those updates, and how their effects can be measured inside the network.

% \subsection{Oscillatory Dynamics, Stability, and Sampling Noise}

Let $\theta_t\in\mathbb{R}^p$ collect the network's $p$ parameters after $t$ completed updates. The gradient $g_t$ is computed from a mini-batch at $t$, before the next update. Plain SGD uses a constant learning rate $\eta>0$ as
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta g_t.
  \label{eq:sgd_map_stability}
\end{equation}
For full-batch GD, the gradient uses the complete training objective $g_t=\nabla\mathcal L(\theta_t)$. The Hessian $H_t:=\nabla^2\mathcal L(\theta_t)$ collects its second derivatives and describes how the loss's slope changes with the parameters. The update-map Jacobian $J_t$ describes, to first order, how a small parameter perturbation changes after one update
\begin{equation}
  J_t=I-\eta H_t.
  \label{eq:jacobian_sgd}
\end{equation}
On a fixed quadratic objective, the Hessian's eigenvectors identify directions whose perturbations evolve independently. The eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is the curvature in direction $i$, and one update multiplies a perturbation in that direction by $1-\eta\lambda_i$. If all these curvatures are positive, every perturbation shrinks precisely when~\cite{polyak1964methods,bertsekas1997nonlinear}
\begin{equation}
  \max_i|1-\eta\lambda_i|<1
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \eta\lambda_{\max}(H_t)<2.
  \label{eq:quad_stability_condition}
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest curvature. If $0<\eta\lambda_i<1$, a perturbation in direction $i$ shrinks without changing sign. If $1<\eta\lambda_i<2$, it changes sign at every step but still shrinks. When $\eta\lambda_i>2$, it changes sign and grows. Thus back-and-forth motion can be either convergent or unstable, even on a fixed quadratic objective.

Different mini-batches can produce different gradients at the same parameters~\cite{mandt2017sgd,hu2019diffusion,jastrzebski2018three}. Write $\xi_t$ for the difference between a batch gradient and the gradient of the full objective. For independent uniform batches of $b$ examples, each sampled without replacement from a dataset of $N>1$ examples,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g_t&=\nabla\mathcal L(\theta_t)+\xi_t,\\
\mathbb E[\xi_t\mid\theta_t]&=0,\\
\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_t\mid\theta_t)
  &=\frac{N-b}{b(N-1)}\Sigma(\theta_t).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sgd_noise_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The expected batch gradient equals the full gradient. Its variability depends on how much the individual example gradients differ. For example $i$ with loss $\ell_i$, let $h_i:=\nabla\ell_i(\theta_t)$ and let $\mu:=N^{-1}\sum_i h_i=\nabla\mathcal L(\theta_t)$ be their mean. The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ records their variation around this mean
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma(\theta_t)
  :=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(h_i-\mu)(h_i-\mu)^\top.
  \label{eq:grad_cov_def}
\end{equation}
In neural networks, however, both $H_t$ and $\Sigma$ change as the parameters move, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:quad_stability_condition} is only an instantaneous, local criterion.

Edge-of-Stability studies show that this distinction matters: full-batch training can keep reducing the loss while sharpness remains near or above $2/\eta$, where a fixed quadratic would not converge~\cite{cohen2021edge,arora2022understanding}. Self-stabilization and Central Flows explain how oscillations interact with curvature and influence slower optimization dynamics~\cite{damian2023self,cohen2025central}. Edge of Stochastic Stability extends the analysis to mini-batch objectives and distinguishes sampling-induced oscillations from instability~\cite{andreyev2025stochastic}. These accounts explain why oscillation and descent can coexist, but describe it through curvature. Path-length studies bound the total distance traveled~\cite{gupta2021path}, but not how much of it successive updates cancel. We relate this cancellation to gradient magnitudes and alignment, both between consecutive updates and across longer separations.

Neural-network Hessians are dominated by a few sharp directions~\cite{sagun2017hessian,ghorbani2019density}, so tracking $\lambda_{\max}$ is informative but requires repeated Hessian--vector products. Bai et al.~\cite{bai2026spikes} explain Adam spikes through decoupling between squared gradients and their running average, and identify this monitoring cost as a scaling limitation. Consecutive-gradient diagnostics already target convergence-stage detection, with known sensitivity to sampling noise~\cite{chee2018diagnostics,pesme2020diagnostics}. Barzilai--Borwein methods provide the classical secant construction underlying our step-direction quantity, which is the reciprocal of BB1 when defined~\cite{barzilai1988two,tan2016bbsgd}. Lee and Lee~\cite{lee2026road} also avoid additional derivative evaluations, using observed loss changes to characterize completed steps. We instead recover step-averaged directional curvature from gradient differences and the applied increment, exactly for smooth, fixed objectives with full gradients, and separately test gradient alignment and amplification as empirical warnings before the next Adam update.

Central Flows explicitly leaves unresolved how loss-landscape dynamics translate into the roles of individual layers~\cite{cohen2025central}. Representation-comparison methods characterize similarities across layers and training states~\cite{raghu2017svcca,kornblith2019similarity}; we examine consecutive representation changes to locate reversal, quantify movement, and identify low-dimensional patterns and shared timing during oscillatory training. Kosson et al.~\cite{kosson2024warmup} identify representation change as a promising measure of update size, but leave its control open; their initial interventions focus on reducing warmup. We test local displacement caps during established oscillatory training. Because loss excursions can accompany improved feature learning~\cite{zhu2024catapults}, our comparisons assess training-loss outcomes alongside fluctuation suppression, asking whether movement control helps while reversals persist.
