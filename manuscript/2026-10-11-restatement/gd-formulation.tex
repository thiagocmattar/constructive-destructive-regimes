\section{Introduction}

Training large language models (LLMs) relies on adaptive optimization, momentum, gradient clipping, and learning-rate schedules to stabilize learning and mitigate phenomena such as loss spikes and oscillatory updates. To better understand the dynamics these mechanisms regulate, we study how gradient alignment evolves over the course of training.

We identify a transition between two distinct regimes. Early in training, successive gradients tend to align, allowing updates to accumulate constructively as the loss decreases. Later, gradient directions reverse more frequently, causing successive updates to partially cancel one another. Despite this increased cancellation, the loss can continue to decrease.

Two complementary intuitions help explain this behavior. From the perspective of representation learning, each new mini-batch updates parameters that also encode previously learned information. Depending on its direction, an update may reinforce existing representations or partially undo earlier changes. From the perspective of optimization geometry, a step may overshoot the bottom of a loss valley, prompting subsequent steps to reverse direction and partially retrace the path. Both mechanisms can give rise to oscillatory parameter updates even as optimization continues to make progress.

We formalize these dynamics using identities for stochastic gradient descent (SGD), relating the alignment of consecutive gradients to curvature and gradient noise, and cumulative update cancellation to net parameter displacement. Building on these relations, we introduce scalable diagnostics for quantifying these dynamics and tracking the transition between constructive and oscillatory regimes during large-scale model training.


\section{Parameter displacement under stochastic gradient descent}

Let $\theta_t\in\mathbb{R}^p$ denote the parameters after update $t$, and let
$g_t$ denote a mini-batch gradient evaluated at $\theta_{t-1}$. We consider
constant-step SGD with learning rate $\eta>0$, without momentum, weight decay,
or clipping:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta g_t.
  \label{eq:sgd}
\end{equation}
All norms below are Euclidean and include the full parameter vector. Full-batch
GD is the special case in which every $g_t$ is the exact training-objective
gradient. The displacement identities in this section hold pathwise, regardless
of how the stochastic gradients are generated.

After $T$ updates, summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd} gives the displacement from
initialization,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_T:=\theta_T-\theta_0=-\eta\sum_{t=1}^T g_t.
  \label{eq:displacement}
\end{equation}
Expanding its squared norm separates individual gradient magnitudes from their
temporal interactions:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\norm{\Delta_T}^2
  =\eta^2\sum_{t=1}^T\norm{g_t}^2
   +2\eta^2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}g_u^\top g_t.}
  \label{eq:energy-interaction}
\end{equation}
The gradient-energy terms are nonnegative. Each pairwise inner product can be
positive, zero, or negative: aligned gradients reinforce their contribution to
the sum, whereas opposing gradients cancel part of it. Gradient energy is not
itself net displacement; the interaction term determines how much of this energy
remains in the squared norm of the accumulated vector. Measuring displacement
relative to initialization also avoids an additional cross term involving
$\theta_0$ that would appear in an expansion of $\norm{\theta_T}^2$.

For nonzero consecutive gradients, define their cosine similarity as
\begin{equation}
  \rho_t:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top g_t}{\norm{g_{t-1}}\norm{g_t}},
  \qquad t=2,\ldots,T.
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Separating adjacent pairs from the remaining interactions yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\norm{\Delta_T}^2={}&\eta^2\sum_{t=1}^T\norm{g_t}^2\\
 &+2\eta^2\underbrace{\sum_{t=2}^T
    \norm{g_{t-1}}\norm{g_t}\rho_t}_{\text{lag-one interaction}}\\
 &+2\eta^2\underbrace{\sum_{t=3}^T\sum_{u=1}^{t-2}
    g_u^\top g_t}_{\text{interaction beyond lag one}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lag-split}
\end{equation}
Negative $\rho_t$ contributes cancellation between consecutive updates. However,
longer-lag interactions can reinforce or offset these negative contributions.
Thus, adjacent anti-alignment diagnoses a local interaction, while the complete
double sum determines cumulative interaction over the chosen horizon. We retain
both terms rather than assume that lag one approximates the whole trajectory.

\section{Curvature, gradient noise, and consecutive alignment}

\subsection{A local expansion along the SGD trajectory}

Let $\Loss$ denote the full training objective. We write a stochastic gradient
as
\begin{equation}
  g_t=\nabla\Loss(\theta_{t-1})+\xi_t,
  \label{eq:noise-model}
\end{equation}
where $\xi_t$ is the gradient estimation error. Suppose $\Loss$ is twice
continuously differentiable along the step under consideration, and define
$H_{t-1}:=\nabla^2\Loss(\theta_{t-2})$. Expanding the full gradient around
$\theta_{t-2}$ and substituting the preceding SGD step gives
\begin{equation}
  g_t=(I-\eta H_{t-1})g_{t-1}+(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})+r_t,
  \label{eq:gradient-evolution}
\end{equation}
where $r_t$ is the Taylor remainder. Neglecting $r_t$ recovers the first-order
approximation. Taking the inner product with $g_{t-1}$ then gives
\begin{equation}
\rho_t\approx
\frac{\norm{g_{t-1}}^2-\eta g_{t-1}^\top H_{t-1}g_{t-1}
      +g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})}
     {\norm{g_{t-1}}\norm{g_t}}.
\label{eq:rho-expanded}
\end{equation}
The numerator contains the preceding gradient energy, a curvature contribution,
and the change in gradient noise. Positive curvature along the preceding
gradient direction reduces its alignment with the next gradient. The noise
difference can reinforce or oppose this effect on an individual step.

We normalize the curvature and noise terms by the preceding gradient energy:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top H_{t-1}g_{t-1}}{\norm{g_{t-1}}^2},
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_t:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})}
                            {\norm{g_{t-1}}^2}.
  \label{eq:kappa-epsilon}
\end{equation}
With these definitions, Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho-expanded} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\rho_t\approx\frac{\norm{g_{t-1}}}{\norm{g_t}}
                   (1-\eta\kappa_t+\varepsilon_t).}
  \label{eq:rho-normalized}
\end{equation}
The norm ratio is positive, so the first-order sign prediction depends on
$1-\eta\kappa_t+\varepsilon_t$ (Table~\ref{tab:sign}). A larger positive
directional curvature favors reversal; noise shifts the corresponding boundary.
These conditions describe alignment, not the separate question of whether an
oscillation is damped or unstable.

\centering

The Taylor remainder is neglected; these are not exact sign equivalences for
an arbitrary nonlinear step.}
\label{tab:sign}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Alignment & Cosine sign & First-order condition\\
\midrule
Positive & $\rho_t>0$ & $\eta\kappa_t<1+\varepsilon_t$\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The remainder matters near this boundary. In fact,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gradient-evolution} implies the exact relation
\begin{equation}
  \rho_t=\frac{\norm{g_{t-1}}}{\norm{g_t}}
          (1-\eta\kappa_t+\varepsilon_t+\delta_t),
  \qquad
  \delta_t:=\frac{g_{t-1}^\top r_t}{\norm{g_{t-1}}^2}.
  \label{eq:remainder}
\end{equation}
The first-order sign is reliable only to the extent that this omitted term does
not change the numerator's sign.

\subsection{Directional curvature and a gradient-derived secant}

The quantity $\kappa_t$ is a Hessian Rayleigh quotient along the preceding
stochastic gradient. If $H_{t-1}v_i=\lambda_i v_i$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis
and $g_{t-1}=\sum_i a_i v_i$, then
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i a_i^2}{\sum_i a_i^2}.
  \label{eq:rayleigh}
\end{equation}
Thus, the gradient selects an eigenvalue-weighted average rather than the
maximum eigenvalue. A sharp direction can exist without contributing much to
the current gradient. This distinction is important when comparing alignment
with the largest Hessian eigenvalue.

Rearranging Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho-normalized} motivates the observable quantity
\begin{equation}
  \eta\widehat\kappa_t
  :=1-\rho_t\frac{\norm{g_t}}{\norm{g_{t-1}}}+\varepsilon_t.
  \label{eq:kappa-estimate}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:remainder} shows that
$\widehat\kappa_t=\kappa_t-\delta_t/\eta$. Consequently, it approximates the
pointwise directional curvature when the Taylor remainder is small. For
mini-batch gradients, the noise term requires additional information and cannot
be recovered from adjacent cosine and norms alone.

For full-batch GD, $\xi_t=0$ and hence $\varepsilon_t=0$. The recorded
gradient-only quantity is then exactly
\begin{equation}
\eta\widehat\kappa_t
 =1-\rho_t\frac{\norm{g_t}}{\norm{g_{t-1}}}
 =-\frac{g_{t-1}^\top(g_t-g_{t-1})}{\norm{g_{t-1}}^2}.
\label{eq:gd-proxy}
\end{equation}
It measures the change in the gradient projected onto its preceding direction.
In exact-arithmetic GD, the fundamental theorem of calculus provides a finite-step
curvature interpretation:
\begin{equation}
\widehat\kappa_t=
 \frac{g_{t-1}^\top\overline H_t g_{t-1}}{\norm{g_{t-1}}^2},
\qquad
\overline H_t=\int_0^1\nabla^2\Loss(\theta_{t-2}-s\eta g_{t-1})\,ds.
\label{eq:integrated-hessian}
\end{equation}
The secant therefore samples curvature averaged along the preceding step. It
equals the pointwise Rayleigh quotient on a quadratic objective, but need not
equal either the local maximum eigenvalue or its finite-step average on a
nonlinear trajectory.

\subsection{The noise contribution under unbiased sampling}

To isolate the stochastic term, assume fresh, conditionally unbiased mini-batch
sampling with finite second moments. Let $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ contain the history
through the preceding update, so that
$\E[\xi_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$. Since $g_{t-1}$ is measurable with respect
to this history,
\begin{equation}
  \E[g_{t-1}^\top\xi_t]=0.
  \label{eq:fresh-noise}
\end{equation}
The preceding gradient already contains $\xi_{t-1}$. Conditional unbiasedness
at that earlier step removes its cross term with
$\nabla\Loss(\theta_{t-2})$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \E[g_{t-1}^\top\xi_{t-1}]=\E[\norm{\xi_{t-1}}^2].
\end{equation}
Subtracting these two expectations yields
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\E[g_{t-1}^\top(\xi_t-\xi_{t-1})]
              =-\E[\norm{\xi_{t-1}}^2]\leq0.}
  \label{eq:noise-expectation}
\end{equation}
Even unbiased noise therefore produces a nonpositive expected contribution to
the unnormalized adjacent inner product in this decomposition. This identifies
a mechanism that can reduce successive-gradient alignment without requiring
large positive directional curvature. It is not a statement that the expected
cosine must be negative: the total numerator has other contributions and the
cosine denominator is random. Correlated sampling or sampling without the stated
conditional unbiasedness need not satisfy this identity.

\section{Normalized displacement and cumulative interference}

Adjacent alignment concerns one pair of steps. To characterize accumulation
over the complete trajectory, we compare the sum of gradient lengths with the
length of their sum:
\begin{equation}
  A_T:=\sum_{t=1}^T\norm{g_t},
  \qquad B_T:=\norm{\sum_{t=1}^T g_t}.
  \label{eq:ab}
\end{equation}
For Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd}, $\eta A_T$ is the discrete path length and
$\eta B_T=\norm{\Delta_T}$ is its endpoint displacement. Provided $A_T>0$,
we define
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{R_T:=\frac{B_T}{A_T}
       =\frac{\norm{\Delta_T}}{\eta\sum_{t=1}^T\norm{g_t}}\in[0,1].}
  \label{eq:normalized-displacement}
\end{equation}
The triangle inequality gives this range. Values near one describe coherent
accumulation; small values indicate that little of the accumulated step length
remains as net parameter motion. A small ratio does not imply small absolute
displacement, because the path length in its denominator can be large.

Expanding $A_T^2$ and $B_T^2$ makes the connection to pairwise directional
disagreement exact:
\begin{equation}
 R_T^2=1-\frac{2\sum_{1\leq u<t\leq T}
        (\norm{g_u}\norm{g_t}-g_u^\top g_t)}
       {\left(\sum_{t=1}^T\norm{g_t}\right)^2}.
 \label{eq:interference-gap}
\end{equation}
Each difference in the numerator is nonnegative by Cauchy--Schwarz. This metric
compares the observed sum with fully aligned accumulation of the same gradient
lengths. It includes orthogonal and partially aligned pairs as well as opposing
pairs; low $R_T$ does not require every interaction to be negative. Similarly,
negative adjacent cosines do not determine the sign of the complete interaction
term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy-interaction}.

The normalized displacement measures the geometry of a trajectory, not its
progress in objective value. Movement can cancel in a sharply curved direction
while continuing to reduce the loss in other directions. The ratio also depends
on the selected horizon: a full-prefix measurement includes the earlier aligned
phase even after a later phase becomes strongly alternating.

\section{Full-batch GD evidence on CIFAR-10}

\subsection{Experimental setup and measurement}

We examine the small CIFAR-10 squared-loss setting used in the released
edge-of-stability experiments~\cite{cohen2021,eoscode}. Cohen et al. describe
a regime in which the maximum training-loss Hessian eigenvalue remains near,
and often slightly above, $2/\eta$, while short-term loss increases coexist
with longer-term loss reduction~\cite{cohen2021}. Our experiment asks how the
gradient secant and cumulative displacement behave alongside this spectral
pattern.

We use the first 5,000 CIFAR-10 training images and a fully connected
$3072$--$200$--$200$--$10$ network with tanh hidden activations and biases
($656{,}810$ parameters). Inputs are centered and standardized per input
coordinate using the complete 50,000-image training split. For one-hot targets
$y_i$ and network outputs $h_\theta(x_i)$, the objective is
\begin{equation}
  \Loss(\theta)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^N
                   \norm{h_\theta(x_i)-y_i}^2,
  \qquad N=5000.
\end{equation}
There is no additional division by the number of classes. Four GD runs share
the same frozen initialization from seed zero and use learning rates
\[
  \eta\in\{0.004,2/300,0.01,0.02\}.
\]
Each run completes 20,000 updates with no momentum, weight decay,
clipping, augmentation, learning-rate schedule, or injected gradient noise.
The full gradient accumulates five physical batches of 1,000 examples before
each update. This is full-batch optimization, not mini-batch sampling.

We record loss and raw-gradient diagnostics at every update. The largest
algebraic Hessian eigenvalue is estimated on all 5,000 examples at initialization
and every 100 updates, including the final state. Each returned eigenpair
passes a relative residual check of at most $0.005$. We explicitly select the
largest algebraic eigenvalue, rather than the largest magnitude; the fixed
20,000-update horizon and frozen initialization are also declared choices of
this reproduction. Parameter updates use FP32, with TF32 disabled and FP64
diagnostic reductions. The displacement identities are stated in exact
arithmetic; actual parameter displacements are separately audited for rounding.

Figure~\ref{fig:gd} shows all recorded values without smoothing or temporal
thinning. The first adjacent pair is defined at update two; an undefined first
value is not replaced by zero. Loss and Hessian values are indexed by parameter
state, while the gradient for update $t$ belongs to $\theta_{t-1}$. These labels
are retained when comparing trends across the horizon. Hessian measurements are
connected for display but remain 100-step samples.

\subsection{Alignment reversal and the spectral regime}

At $\eta=0.004$, consecutive gradients remain almost parallel throughout the
run. The gradient-derived $\eta\widehat\kappa_t$ remains close to zero, whereas
$\eta\lambda_{\max}$ increases to approximately $1.68$. This separation is
consistent with the distinction between gradient-direction curvature and
maximum curvature; the secant is not a general estimator of the largest
Hessian eigenvalue.

The three larger rates show a different pattern. The first negative adjacent
pair occurs at updates $9467$, $4565$, and $1156$ for $\eta=2/300$, $0.01$, and
$0.02$, respectively. Their late cosine means are approximately $-0.999$,
and their final scaled largest eigenvalues lie between $2.070$ and $2.077$.
Thus, the alignment reversal moves earlier as the learning rate increases
within this matched cohort. The loss develops short-term fluctuations but
continues to decrease over the fixed horizon.

In this alternating regime, $\eta\widehat\kappa_t$ fluctuates near two
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gd}c). Equation~\eqref{eq:gd-proxy} explains this value directly:
if $\rho_t\approx-1$ and consecutive gradient norms are similar, then
$1-\rho_t\norm{g_t}/\norm{g_{t-1}}\approx2$. The secant therefore tracks the
late edge-of-stability scale in these runs and supplies a directional
description of the reversal. It does not follow the entire maximum-eigenvalue
curve pointwise. Moreover, the coincidence near two is linked algebraically
to the measured reversal; it is not an independent proof of stability.

For a positive quadratic eigendirection of curvature $\lambda$, plain GD
multiplies that component by $1-\eta\lambda$ at each step. Alignment reverses
when $\eta\lambda>1$, while contraction requires $0<\eta\lambda<2$.
The values one and two therefore describe different local questions:
directional sign reversal and contraction of a fixed quadratic mode. The
nonlinear training trajectory need not obey a frozen-quadratic stability
interpretation globally.

\subsection{Cumulative cancellation despite continued loss reduction}

The adjacent reversal coincides with a sharp decline in normalized displacement
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gd}e). At the horizon, $B_T/A_T$ is $0.7602$ for $\eta=0.004$,
but only $0.0250$, $0.0148$, and $0.0105$ at the three larger rates. Hence,
only about $2.50\%$, $1.48\%$, and $1.05\%$ of the accumulated path length
remains as net displacement in these runs. This connects the local reversal
diagnostic with substantial cumulative cancellation.

Absolute displacement does not approach zero in this comparison. The audited
endpoint distances from initialization are approximately $3.96$, $5.09$,
$5.99$, and $7.70$, in increasing learning-rate order. At the same time, the
corresponding path lengths are approximately $5.21$, $203.80$, $403.96$, and
$732.59$. The small ratios arise because net motion is small relative to the
much longer paths. The complete raw-gradient all-pair interaction also remains
positive in all four runs, despite the strongly negative late adjacent pairs
at the larger rates. Longer-lag interactions cannot therefore be omitted when
interpreting Eq.~\eqref{eq:lag-split}.

Raw-gradient norms fall during the early phase, then increase and fluctuate
after the reversal at the larger learning rates (Fig.~\ref{fig:gd}f).
Larger step lengths can then contribute little additional net motion because
successive directions oppose one another. Oscillation across a curved basin is
one possible interpretation, but the scalar diagnostics do not identify a
spatial spiral or establish that mechanism. Neither a large gradient norm nor
a small displacement ratio alone demonstrates loss instability.

Finally, stronger cancellation does not imply slower convergence in this
finite-horizon comparison. The final training loss decreases from $0.09688$ at
$\eta=0.004$ to $0.01945$ at $\eta=0.02$. The geometric loss of net path
efficiency coexists with greater training-loss reduction. These observations
are based on one frozen initialization, a preselected learning-rate grid and
a finite horizon; they do not establish a general optimizer ranking or a
generalization result.
